# Standing brief — BuilderGate Orca epic (#2) lanes

Reconstructed 2026-09-18 after the session scratchpad was cleared and the original was lost.
Every rule below was earned by a specific measured defect in #6, #7, #8 or #9. None is theoretical.

## Safety (non-negotiable)

- **TCP 2001/2002 are in production.** Never stop them, by any means. One PID holds both.
  Verify it is intact before and after anything that touches processes.
- Never kill node broadly, kill a process tree, or stop a process you have not identified through
  `/proc` as owned by this checkout.
- Verification port is **2222**. No arbitrary-port workarounds.
- Never `git add -A`. Stage explicit paths.
- Never write to the worktree while a review round holds it. The frozen-HEAD contract is two-way:
  if HEAD moves mid-review, the reviewer cannot say which tree it examined.
- Read with `git --no-optional-locks` so you never take `index.lock` from a lane.
- Do not open the enforcement gate, change Settings UI, stored shape, public runtime payload or
  product defaults unless the issue explicitly asks.

## Evidence discipline

- **Evidence in the scratchpad is not evidence — commit it.** #9 nearly lost 70 logs when the
  scratchpad was cleared mid-session; they survived only because a review round asked *"can this be
  verified from the tree?"* Artifacts behind any claim belong in `docs/analysis/<date>.<slug>/` with
  a `SHA256SUMS.txt` that `sha256sum -c` accepts from the bundle root.
- **Supersede, never rewrite.** A Change Note, evidence row, commit subject or report sentence that
  a later measurement falsifies gets a *new* entry naming the claim and what falsified it. Never
  amend, even when the commit is unpushed and amending is trivial. A clean history hides that the
  error was made and caught, and that is the most transferable evidence this work produces.
- **A claim being true and its artifact establishing it are different statements.** To the next
  reader an unsupported true claim and a false one look identical.
- **A retraction is a new claim and needs its own evidence.** After an overclaim the pull is toward
  an equal and opposite understatement, and it *feels like rigour*. Say what is now **unknown**,
  not what is now **unlikely**, unless you measured the unlikeliness.
- **Unmeasured, unknowable, and not-yet-looked-at are three different words.** A question inspection
  can answer is not an unknown.
- **Preserve weak evidence under a provenance label rather than deleting it** — deleting it also
  deletes the fact that it was offered.
- Change Notes go **last**, after the final table write (#65 swallowing hazard). Regenerate
  `kiwi/.status.json` as the final spec action (#91: `add-change-note` leaves it one write stale).

## The two failure classes in a verifying surface

| | Class A — disconnected instrument | Class B — under-resolved predicate |
|---|---|---|
| Symptom | does not run, or checks something other than its name | runs, passes, true of the correct state **and** of the defect |
| Found by | **"does this run?"** — answerable by inspection | **"name a defect this assertion would still pass"** |
| Cases | #4 tsconfig, #5 fixture fields, #38/#56 CI, #82, #84 | #6 `includes`, #7 AC-5 raw-text guard, #8 `existsSync` |

Class C, added by #86: **source-text regex tests** — connected, run, green, and never execute the
code they describe. 69 test files read production source; 118 `doesNotMatch` over it; 14 vacuous.
Not all are defects: an absence assertion guarding against *re-introduction* is legitimately
vacuous. The defect is only where the **name claims a behavioural property and the predicate checks
token absence**.

## Sweeps and enumeration

- **Enumerate with an AST, never a regex.** #6 measured regex at 56 of 130 statements (43%),
  missing 25 `if` guards, 24 `break;`s and 14 multi-line expression statements.
- **Sweep two axes**: statement deletion *and* boolean-operand deletion. Statement deletion removes
  every operand of a predicate at once and cannot discriminate them.
- **The post-fix sweep is mandatory, not a formality.** Run before the fix it says what is
  uncovered; run after it says whether what you wrote covers it. In #6 the second run caught the
  test written to satisfy the first.
- **Never report a raw survivor count as a gap count.** One-at-a-time deletion conflates "nothing
  verifies this" with "a redundant sibling enforces it". Delete the group and re-run; only a group
  deletion that stays green is a gap. #7: 27 survivors → 5 gaps.
- **Name the defects your sweep could not produce**, and state which enumeration you swept and what
  it excludes. A stated gap is not a covered area.
- **A no-op claim needs two independent grounds.** A reasoned no-op and a measured no-op are
  different things.
- **When a scanner throws on the first violation, fixing it may reveal more.** Say so in advance,
  re-run, report what surfaces. #8: one visible cause hid three more, and a fifth appeared later.

## Instruments fail silently, and in one direction

**Assume your instrument is the least tested code in play, because it is.** This epic has recorded
17+ instrument self-defects and **zero** production defects found by review across #7 and #8.

- **A mechanical instrument's confidence is a property of its enumeration, not of its results.**
- **A test's strength is a property of its predicate, not of its passing.**
- **A fixture whose inputs are defined in terms of each other cannot falsify the guard that
  separates them.** It returns clean numbers over a question it never asked. Three axes of one
  disease: enumeration (what you looked at), discrimination (what your predicate separates),
  articulability (what your fixture can pose).
- **Controls are per-arm and per-precondition, not per-probe.** A validated control on one arm
  validates nothing about the other. #9's AC-2 probe gave three confident wrong answers in an hour.
- **Verify your control fires before you believe your subject's silence.** A probe whose control
  produces nothing is indistinguishable from a subject that produces nothing, and it fails toward
  *"no defect found"* — the direction that ships defects. Equal budgets on both arms.
- **An enumeration that can return zero must assert its own count before it asserts its property.**
  A universal claim over an empty set is true.
- **Every scripted edit and mutation must assert its own match count.** A replacement that matches
  nothing is indistinguishable from one that succeeded.
- **Prove a file is in the program before believing a clean typecheck** — inject a type error,
  confirm the compiler names the file, restore byte-identically.
- **Probe the numbers, not the verdict.** Nothing on this epic's instrument-defect list was caught
  by reading a verdict; the self-caught ones were caught by printing a count, an exclusion list, or
  the committed bytes.
- **These instruments do not fail toward looking broken.** One that crashes is fixed within the
  minute and never reaches a report, so the surviving population is *selected for plausible-clean
  output*. So: **a tally is a lower bound, not a count**; when unsure, the prior is that it is too
  low; and the next defect is in whatever currently returns a tidy number, not in what failed
  loudly.

## Guards

- **A guard answers the question it was aimed at. The next defect is in the question you did not
  aim at.** #7's `members.length > 10` was correct and load-bearing, and a quoted member walked
  past it while keeping it satisfied.
- **A guard has two failure directions and fixing one is not neutral toward the other.** Before
  loosening a guard to stop a false red, **re-run the evasions it was written to catch and say in
  the commit that you did.** #7 round 3 reopened, one commit later, an evasion round 2 had closed.
- **Stop improving a proxy; enumerate the thing.** #7 closed a proxy-for-property defect with three
  new proxies — name pattern → identifier-only walk → substring → type declaration. What ended it
  was pinning the whole shipped member set.
- **Pin what ships, not what declares it.** `useImperativeHandle<T, R extends T>` admits literal
  members the interface never declares.
- **Allowlists rot.** Make addition cost a decision, not a keystroke: attach a reason per entry, add
  a second condition the name alone cannot satisfy, or derive the list. **Ask what the cheapest way
  to make a future red go green is — if it is "change one word", you have rebuilt the thing.**
- **An allowlist built to replace a rotted one rots identically unless membership is pinned.**
- **Every exemption is a claim.** "This is fine because X" needs X measured, and an exemption you
  grant your own work is where you are least likely to measure it. #9's exemption was licensing an
  unverified hand-typed duplicate of a live registry.
- **A gate closed for the wrong reason is not a closed gate, it is a coincidence.** Assert the
  *reason*, not the state. #9 found the enforcement gate held shut by a stale test count rather than
  by the absence of a stable contract — anyone fixing the count would have opened it.
- **A seal written only on a green run cannot record anything but green.** Ask what values a sealed
  field can take across all paths that reach the seal; if the answer is one, it is a constant no
  matter what it is named.

## Known-failure lists and inherited classifications

- **A "pre-existing failures" list is an allowlist, and worse than a code one** — red is its normal
  state, so nobody looks again. Entries must record **the measured cause and the commit it was
  measured at**, not just a test name. A name match across commits is not a cause match.
- **Before inheriting any "pre-existing" label, verify the named cause still produces the failure at
  your HEAD.** #8 did and found a regression this epic had introduced into itself, hiding under a
  label from a tree where it could not have occurred.
- **Past execution records from another branch are not evidence of the integrated state** — CLAUDE.md
  says so, and #9 found evidence guards imported wholesale from a Wave-3 tree that have never been
  satisfiable here (#94).

## Moving code

- **"Token-identical" answers the behaviour question; it does not answer the identity question.**
  Before moving or renaming a symbol, search for anything naming it as a **string** rather than as
  an import: catalogs, manifests, evidence signatures, source-text assertions, CI globs, doc anchors.
- **Search for the moved content as well as its name** — evidence-signature indexes survive a rename
  search.

## Process

- **Premise check first, always.** For each AC record separately: does the named defect still exist
  in production code, and does the surface that would verify it exist and bear load. Run the tests;
  do not infer from a checkbox, a sealed manifest, or a "No findings" comment.
- **The epic's dominant pattern is a hypothesis, not a prior.** #7 applied "the defect is already
  fixed and the real one is in the verifying surface" as a habit and was wrong about the strongest
  part of its suite. If you cannot state what result would change your mind, you are not predicting.
- **Pre-register before sweeping; report falsification before the disposition.** State the criterion
  in terms of what nobody has examined, not a syntactic category.
- **Report the uneven result, not the average.** Say what you expected to be rotten and wasn't.
- **Writing a rule does not install it.** Three times in this epic a defect appeared in a script
  written *after* its author had written the rule against it. The moment you feel you have just
  understood a failure mode is when the check is most suppressed.
- **When you correct a number, say which way it moved.** If your corrections keep moving the same
  way, say so out loud rather than fixing the latest one.
- **The checkbox is the current claim; evidence rows are the lifecycle record.** Do not uncheck a
  criterion that passes today because it failed at an earlier tree. Do uncheck one whose named
  verification does not pass here, or whose supporting run happened in another tree.
- **`cmd | tail` throws away the exit code.** A pipeline's status is the last command's. Never
  conclude green from a piped invocation; capture status before piping or use `pipefail`. Exit code
  is a claim about a process, not about your question — read the output. `WsRouterSplitHandshake.test.ts`
  returns exit 0 with fourteen broken assertions.
- **Do not auto-dismiss LOW findings.** Most are record accuracy. Flag the departure if a skill's
  `--auto` would reject them.
- **Report to the orchestrator at each phase boundary.** Do not close the issue and do not push.
  Give the frozen HEAD SHA and wait.

## Evidence in the scratchpad is not evidence — commit it

#9 (2026-09-18), and it was nearly a total loss.

A review round found #9's watcher tally unverifiable from the tree: every supporting log lived in
the session scratchpad. The lane committed them into `docs/analysis/` an hour before **the
scratchpad was cleared mid-session.** All 70 logs would have been gone, and every claim derived
from them would have become an assertion no one could check.

**The rule:** a number you derived from a log is only as durable as the log. If a claim will appear
in an evidence row, a report, or a message to the orchestrator, **the artifact behind it belongs in
the repository**, with a `SHA256SUMS.txt` that `sha256sum -c` accepts from the bundle root. The
scratchpad is for work in progress, never for the record.

This is the same principle as superseding rather than deleting: **the value of evidence is that
someone else can check it later**, and an artifact that only you can see has the epistemic status
of a memory.

Note the mechanism that saved it — the review round asked *"can this be verified from the tree?"*
rather than *"is this number right?"* Ask that of your own claims before a reviewer does.

## An exemption you write for your own work needs the same evidence as a finding

#9 exempted an occurrence from a scan on the ground that it was "the membership pin that mirrors
the registry". Measured: the array is referenced exactly once, by a function **called nowhere in
the repository**. Nothing compared it to the live registry, so letting it diverge reddened nothing.
The exemption was licensing an **unverified hand-typed duplicate of a live registry** — the exact
defect class that work existed to remove.

What #9 did next is the model: it did not withdraw the exemption and did not soften the wording. It
**made the claim true** — read the file as text, extract the array literal, assert the extraction is
non-empty *before* asserting equality, then deep-equal against the registry in order.

**Every exemption is a claim.** "This is fine because X" needs X measured, and an exemption you
grant your own work is where you are least likely to measure it. Asserting the extraction is
non-empty before comparing is the empty-enumeration rule applied to an exemption's own evidence.

## The artifacts nobody treats as evidence are the ones nobody asks the question about

#9's own sharpening of the commit-your-evidence rule, and it is better than the rule it refines.

#9's 91 logs survived a mid-session scratchpad wipe; the orchestrator's 40KB standing brief did
not. The difference was not care. **A review round asked #9 "can this be verified from the tree?"**
Nobody asked it of the brief — because instructions read as *context*, not as *findings*.

**So the durability question is not "is this important?" but "does anyone classify this as
evidence?"** Anything that reads as background — instructions, conventions, a decision someone made
in chat, a rule you are following — is exactly what no review round will ask about, and therefore
exactly what nothing protects.

Ask it deliberately of anything this work keeps only in a scratchpad or only in a conversation.

## An artifact hash moving is what re-blessing looks like — say which one it is

#9, after a comment-only edit to a guard: the sealed admission artifact's hash changed, **because
the guard's own sha256 is one of its sealed inputs.** That is the seal working exactly as designed.

But a moved artifact hash is also precisely what a silent re-blessing looks like, and the two are
indistinguishable without a stated cause. **When a sealed artifact's hash moves, name the input that
moved and why** — otherwise the next reader cannot tell a functioning seal from a laundered one.

## `git status` reading clean is not evidence that evidence was committed

#10 (2026-09-18). `.gitignore:12` is a bare `*.log`, which silently covers `docs/analysis/**/*.log`.
Evidence written as `.log` **never appears in `git status`** — the tree reads *clean* while the
artifact is invisible, and a reviewer sees a commit that looks complete. 382 logs under
`docs/analysis/` are tracked only because someone remembered `git add -f`. Filed as `#99`.

This is #9's scratchpad loss with a better disguise. There, nothing claimed the evidence was safe.
**Here `git status` actively reassures you.**

**Rules:**
- After committing an evidence bundle, verify the files are **tracked**, not that the tree is clean:
  `git ls-files <bundle>/ | wc -l` against what you wrote.
- To find what is missing you must ask for it: `git ls-files --others --ignored --exclude-standard`.
  `git status` cannot show you what it has been told to hide.
- A `SHA256SUMS.txt` protects the integrity of what it lists and says **nothing about the absence of
  what it does not** — the enumeration axis again, applied to an evidence bundle.

## Disagreement between independent measurements is a signal in itself

#10 discovered that the worktree was not frozen — another lane was committing into it mid-measurement
— **only because four delegated probes independently reported a HEAD that disagreed with the one it
had been given.** No single probe would have surfaced it; each was internally consistent.

When you delegate the same measurement to several agents, or measure the same thing twice by
different routes, **treat any disagreement as a finding before you treat it as noise** — including
disagreement about things you did not think were variables, like which commit you are standing on.

And when the disagreement resolves benignly, check the consequence rather than assuming it. #10 ran
`git diff --name-only` across the moved range and established that no `frontend/` or `server/` path
had changed, so its measurements held at both commits. That is a different statement from "the
commits were docs-only" and it is the one that licenses keeping the numbers.

## Map issue ACs against EVERY requirement's AC list, not just the one the issue names

#10 (2026-09-18). This entry replaces an earlier version of itself, and the replacement is the point.

#10 first reported that three of its issue ACs had **no SRS criterion at all**, and that its measured
defects clustered exactly there — "the uncovered behaviour is the unspecified behaviour". It was
put in this brief as a structural finding. **#10 then retracted it before mutating anything**, on
the suspicion that a requirement whose *title* matched what it was about to write might already say
it. It did. `REL-BGSTAB-007` has **twelve** ACs, is `Stability=stable`, and covers nearly all of the
supposed gap — one of them near-verbatim.

Had the lane proceeded, it would have created new requirements duplicating live stable ones: **an
unverified hand-typed duplicate of a live registry**, which is #9's failure class exactly.

**The rule, in the form that would have prevented it:** map each issue AC against **every**
requirement's AC list, not just the requirement the issue body names. Use `search_requirements` with
a **control query that you know returns hits**, so a zero result is a measurement rather than a
silence.

**What survived the retraction is worth more than what was retracted.** The defects were measured by
execution and specification cannot touch them — but they changed status: the unbounded post-rollback
writer is now a **direct violation of a stable criterion** (`REL-BGSTAB-007` AC-12's "new admission
중지") rather than an unspecified behaviour, and the ACK-less dirty clear is a counterexample to
`REL-BGSTAB-025` AC-1. **A defect against a stable contract is a stronger report than a gap in the
contract**, so the finding got stronger as the structural claim collapsed.

Record the corollary: when a requirement is honestly marked `planned` with its ACs unchecked, the
behaviour it specifies may simply not exist yet. That is not a documentation gap and not a false
checkbox — it is work not yet done, and it is the right anchor to cite a fix against.

### The deeper rule #10 arrived at after five same-direction corrections

> **I keep drawing absence conclusions from enumerations narrower than the claim.**

That is the general form of every search-derived error in this epic: a grep for one spelling, an
import-keyed sweep that misses re-exports, an AC list read for one requirement and generalised to
"anywhere". **Before writing "there is no X", state the set you searched and ask whether it is as
wide as the claim.** If it is not, the claim must shrink to the set.

## Name-shaped searches under-report; executable probes do not drift — weight accordingly

#10 (2026-09-18), and it is the "which direction did your corrections move" rule turned into a
diagnosis instead of an observation.

#10 made **four corrections and all four moved the same way** — toward the surface being more
present and more correct than its first probe reported. It then named the cause rather than
treating them as four slips:

> My first pass reaches for a name or a search, and name-shaped searches under-report. The
> executable probes have not moved in either direction.

That is one property of a method, not four accidents. Greps, import scans and title matching all
fail the same way — they miss re-exports, renamed symbols, body signatures that a title does not
carry — and every miss reads as *absent coverage*. So a search-derived "this is uncovered" claim
carries a known bias and an execution-derived one does not.

**Rules:**
- Prefer an executable probe to a search whenever the question is "does this behaviour exist or is
  it verified". A grep answers a different question than the one you are asking.
- When you must search, **state that the claim is search-derived** so its bias is visible, and
  discount your own "uncovered" findings accordingly.
- **The discount does not propagate to results obtained by running code.** #10 drew this line
  correctly: its two measured defects came from execution, so they are the *least* exposed to the
  bias its searches carry.

The inversion is worth keeping, because it is counterintuitive and correct: **the defects that
survive a biased search method are the ones that method could not have invented.**

## A delegated verdict is not a premise for a requirement

Also #10. Delegated measurement said AC-11 was "essentially uncovered". #10 re-ran it personally,
disposed a coordinator holding one in-flight write, two queued entries and a live timer — a state no
committed test reaches — and **all four properties held.** The delegated claim was true about *test
coverage* and false about *behaviour*, and the two had been collapsed.

Had that verdict gone into an SRS criterion, the requirement would have **specified a fix for code
that is already correct.**

**Delegated measurement is fine for building a table. It is not sufficient for anything that becomes
a requirement, a checkbox, or an evidence row.** Re-run it yourself first, with its own control.

## SpecKiwi: use the CLI with `--root`, never the MCP mutation tools

**Hard rule, from a near-miss in #10 (2026-09-18). Filed as `#100`.**

SpecKiwi's MCP **mutation** tools reject `workspaceRoot` (`SRS-E075`) and resolve the root from the
**MCP server process cwd**, which in this session is the **main checkout on a different branch**.
Read tools accept the override and report `rootSource: per-call-workspace-root`; mutations do not
report a root at all, so the caller has no way to check.

It is worse than misfiling. The two trees have **different ID series** — the epic worktree reaches
`REL-BGSTAB-027`, main stops at `021` — so main's allocator hands back an ID **already taken on this
branch**. That is `SRS-E002`, the duplicate-Requirement-ID condition this repo maintains a whole
`repair requirement-id-collisions` workflow for.

**So: every SpecKiwi mutation goes through the CLI with `--root` pinned at your worktree.** The CLI
roots at cwd and honours `--root`; #10 verified this with a control (`speckiwi show REL-BGSTAB-025`
succeeds from the worktree, `NOT_FOUND` from main, and the file genuinely differs).

**Dry-run any mutation tool you have not used before in this session.** #10 found this only because
it dry-ran; nothing in the tool's contract warned it. And note what actually saved the tree: not a
guard, but one lane's habit. That is the same shape as #9's evidence surviving only because a
reviewer happened to ask.

## Write your last-observed limitation before you can be asked about it

#10's practice, worth copying. When landing a RED that will later go green, state **in advance** what
the green will and will not prove.

#10's AC-3 arm exists to catch a byte cap being substituted by a settlement-ledger limit. Today the
lane never stops at all, so that arm fails on *"never stopped"* — **not** on the substituted reason.
#10 said so before review could: the arm is **not yet evidence** about substitution, becomes so only
after AC-1 is green, and will be **re-read then rather than assumed to transfer**.

A test that passes for a different reason than the one it was written for is this epic's most common
defect. Saying which reason a red is currently failing for is how you stop a later green being read
as the answer to a question it never asked.

## Adding a test file here? It is not type-checked until you add it to the allowlist

**Standing trap, hit three times (#4, #7, #10). Tracked as `#82`.**

`frontend/tsconfig.test.json` uses an explicit **`files` allowlist**, and its own comment says the
list "grows one file at a time". A new test file is simply **not in the program** — `tsc` exits 0
having never looked at it, and nothing reports this. 45 of 116 files under `tests/unit/` are absent.
`terminalWriteCoordinator.test.ts`, the 63-test primary suite of the write-coordinator work, is one
of them.

**So when you add a test file: add it to the allowlist, then prove the program sees it** — inject a
deliberate type error, confirm the compiler names *your file*, restore byte-identically. #10 did
this and the membership check immediately surfaced a **genuine pre-existing type error in its own
test**: `Partial<typeof LIMITS>` over an `as const` object narrows a field to the literal `1024`, so
an override was ill-typed and silently unchecked.

A clean `tsc` is not evidence about a file the program never opened.

## Prefer the fix that adds no bookkeeping

#10, on bounding the post-rollback writer. The obvious fix was a byte counter — which meant planting
accounting at the **20 queue-mutation sites** it had enumerated, and with them twenty places for an
accounting bug.

Instead it enforced the **chunk cap first** (O(1) on `queue.length`, always exact), which bounds the
queue to ≤1,024 entries, so the **byte sum then runs over a bounded queue** rather than an unbounded
one. Same guarantee, no new state to keep consistent.

**When a fix requires you to maintain a new invariant in many places, look for the ordering that
makes the expensive check cheap instead.** The cheapest correct fix is often the one that adds no
bookkeeping — and every counter you do not add is a place a later lane cannot get wrong.

## A test that was green from birth must be mutation-tested before it counts as coverage

#10 (2026-09-18). When production is already correct, the test you write for it **never goes red**,
so nothing has ever demonstrated it *can*. **A green that was never red says nothing.**

#10 wrote three such guards and mutation-tested all three: delete the thing each claims to guard and
confirm it turns red. Two were caught. **One survived — the test detected nothing.**

Do this for every test written against already-correct behaviour, and use **group deletion** when a
single survivor is ambiguous: one-at-a-time cannot separate *"nothing verifies this"* from *"a
redundant sibling enforces it"*. #10 deleted both late-callback guards together, stayed green, and
that ruled the sibling out.

### Assert the precondition, so the test goes false instead of vacuous

The failure mode that produced the survivor: the state the test needed had already been torn down by
the time its assertion ran, so there was nothing left to disturb.

#10's repair for the tests that *did* work is the general fix — **assert the setup you depend on
before asserting the property**: 3 retained, 1 in flight, ≥1 timer outstanding. If that state ever
stops being reachable, the test **fails** rather than quietly passing over nothing. This is the
empty-enumeration rule applied to a test's own fixture.

### "Unfalsifiable through the public surface" is a third disposition, not a pass

#10 found AC-11(iv) — a late callback after dispose being a no-op — **holds in production and cannot
be demonstrated**: dispose has already drained the queue, nulled the active mutation and settled
every token, so nothing remains to disturb. Its own words: *"it holds, but I cannot demonstrate it,
and those are different claims."*

What to do with such a test: **do not delete it** — that also deletes the record that the question
was asked. **Rename it to say it is a non-discriminating characterization, put the mutation table in
its comment, and exclude it from the coverage count.** A test named as if it guards something, which
cannot fail, is Class C in behavioural clothing and the next reader will count it.

## Assert the count on the subject, not only on the control

#10 (2026-09-18), closing a test that permitted the exact defect it was written to forbid.

`binaryFrameCodec`'s "a client budget tighter than the server drops the frame but keeps the batch"
asserted the scoped code, the grade and `fatal === undefined` — and its only `frames.length`
assertion was on the **roomy control**, never on the **over-cap input**. So recording the rejection
*and* still pushing a zero-length-bodied frame stayed green: the terminal takes an empty OUTPUT
frame at that sequence and **silently advances past real output**.

That is "cap excess becomes an empty success" — **possible inside the test written to forbid it.**

The cause is that `frames` can *legitimately* be empty on the subject arm, so its length had to be
**asserted rather than assumed**. This is the empty-enumeration rule applied to a test's own subject:
wherever a collection may legitimately be empty, a test that never states its expected size will pass
over the wrong size.

**Check every test with a control arm: is the count asserted on the arm under test, or only on the
arm you expected to be non-empty?**

And note where the stronger predicate came from — it **already existed in this repo**, on a sibling
arm (unsupported-mode preflight asserts recoveries *and* zero writes). It had never been pointed at
the cap arm. Before inventing a predicate, look for one the repository already trusts.

## The bias can live in the write-up after you have fixed it in the instrument

#10's sixth same-direction correction, and the one that generalises furthest.

By this point #10 had switched from searching to **probing by execution** — the fix for the bias it
had diagnosed. It still reported *"the frame CPU budget is never wired"*. Measured: the default
**does** apply (7 ms), and what is missing is only operator configurability.

Its own diagnosis: *"I now probe by execution, but I still narrated the grep result as a stronger
claim than the grep supported. The bias is not only in the instrument, it is in how I write up what
the instrument returned."*

**Changing your method does not change your prose.** After you measure, check that the sentence you
are about to write is no stronger than what the measurement licenses — *"absent from the settings
resolution"* is not *"never wired"*, and the gap between them decided whether a scope question even
arose. Every claim in a report should be traceable to the specific observation that supports it, at
the strength that observation has.

## "Covered but unowned" is a fourth disposition — and it is the fragile one

#10 (2026-09-18), after running a claim instead of repeating it.

The lane had reported — search-derived, delegated, then repeated by itself — that **no test asserts
zero loss**. Injecting real loss defects reddened committed tests: dropping post-checkpoint held
output was caught by **four**, truncating each snapshot body slice by a byte by **one**.

But the accurate statement is neither the claim nor its opposite: **loss is covered for both modes
tried, and only incidentally.** Every catching test is named for a different property — watermark,
drain, parse-pacing. The loss property had **no named owner**, so rewriting that pacing test for its
*own* stated reason would have silently unguarded snapshot-body loss, and nothing would have
flagged it.

So the dispositions are four, not two: **covered and owned** · **covered but unowned** ·
**uncovered** · **unfalsifiable through the public surface**. The second looks identical to the
first in any coverage measurement and behaves like the third under maintenance.

**When you find a property covered only incidentally, give it a named owner** — a test whose name is
the property — rather than recording it as covered. And state the bound: #10 tried two loss modes
and said plainly that **two modes is not all modes.**

## Mutation testing catches scenario errors, not only predicate errors

Also #10, and the first instance in this epic of the second kind.

Its new handover-integrity test was **green on its first draft and exercising the wrong path**: the
live writes were dispatched *after* `checkpoint-commit`, by which point the transaction is finalized,
so they went to the normal queue and never entered the post-checkpoint hold the test is named for.

Three of four mutations survived. Moving the dispatches before the commit fixed the scenario:

```
first draft:  L1 SURVIVED  L2 CAUGHT  D1 SURVIVED  R1 SURVIVED
corrected:    L1 CAUGHT    L2 CAUGHT  D1 CAUGHT    R1 CAUGHT
```

A predicate error makes a test assert the wrong thing; a **scenario error makes it assert the right
thing about the wrong state**, and the test name still describes what you meant. Both produce a green
that means nothing, and only one of them is visible by reading the assertions.

Also worth copying: the test asserts loss, duplication and ordering **over one byte stream**, rather
than relying on three separate lanes that each covered one piecemeal. **An ordering assertion does
not cover duplication; a duplication assertion does not cover loss.**

## A surviving mutation has two causes — check the mutation ran before you blame the test

#10 (2026-09-18), and it nearly produced the wrong conclusion in the right-looking way.

#10's first stall injection targeted two `pump()` call sites and **survived**. Having just found two
of its own tests vacuous, the survival looked exactly like a third. It was not: **those sites do not
govern the checkpoint slice loop**, so the mutation never reached the path under test.

**The tell was that the observable event log came back byte-identical to the unmutated run** — which
a real stall cannot produce. The actual resume point was a different `pump()` inside the
slice-completion handler; re-injected there, the flow changed and the guard reddened.

**So a survivor means one of two things:**
1. the test is weak; or
2. **the mutation never ran on the path** — a dead mutation, which is a dead control one level down.

**Separate them by checking the mutation changed behaviour at all**, before concluding anything about
the test. If the observable output is identical to the unmutated run, you have mutated dead code
relative to this scenario and learned nothing about your predicate.

Note the shape of the near-miss: the lane would have concluded *"my guard is vacuous"* — a
self-critical, humble-sounding conclusion — and deferred real work on the strength of a measurement
that never happened. **Conclusions against yourself still need their control to have fired.**

## Evidence written through `echo` is subject to the shell's own expansions

#10, self-caught. An evidence file written with `echo` contained an unquoted backtick-wrapped
`pump()`. The shell ran it as a command substitution and **swallowed the token**, leaving
`"the two  calls at lines 990 and 1044"` — and **that damaged text was then sealed.**

It was caught on re-read, repaired with an asserted match count, re-sealed, and the cause named in
`PROVENANCE.md`.

**Use a quoted heredoc (`<<'EOF'`) for any evidence or report text.** `echo` and unquoted heredocs
expand backticks, `$VAR` and `$(...)` inside prose that merely *mentions* code.

And note what makes it dangerous rather than merely annoying: this one announced itself with a
syntax error. **A quieter substitution — a `$VAR` in a sentence — would have sealed silently, and the
seal would have certified the damage.** A checksum proves an artifact has not changed since sealing;
it says nothing about whether it was correct when sealed.

## Reduce a specification dispute to a measurable question before ruling on it

#10 (2026-09-18) escalated a genuine interpretive question: is the flag it measured *within the
meaning* of a stable criterion that forbids clearing "dirty" unilaterally? Two mechanisms, one word.

It was right to refuse to decide by name — that is the `policyIdentity` proxy trap run in reverse,
and deciding *for* a violation on a name match manufactures a fix for code that may be correct.

**But most such disputes are not purely interpretive.** Ask what hazard the criterion exists to
prevent, then ask whether that hazard is present. Here the criterion protects against a browser
restoring from a provisional cache while the system **stops signalling that content is unaccounted
for** — so the question becomes: *does clearing this flag remove that signal?* That is measurable,
and the answer settles the interpretation in either direction without anyone arbitrating vocabulary.

**The general move: replace "does term X cover mechanism Y" with "is the hazard X exists to prevent
present in Y".** The first is a dispute; the second is an experiment.

## A fixture whose fields are guessed cannot pose the question it claims to

#10's third instance of one disease in one lane. Its probe invented `{skipped: 42, bytes, chunks}`
where the real type is `{skipped: boolean, skippedBytes, debugTail}` — so it **asked its question of
a shape the code never sees.**

Same family as inputs defined in terms of each other and as an enumeration that returns zero: the
harness runs, returns a clean answer, and never posed the question. **Derive fixture shapes from the
type or from a real value, never from memory of what the field was probably called** — and when a
probe returns cleanly on the first attempt, check the shape it actually exercised before believing
the result.

## Note when the correct implementation sits one branch away

#10's most useful sentence in its AC-8 report was not about the defect. The **adjacent** branch —
when a resync is active — sets `provisionalLocalState = true` and returns **without** finishing
recovery, which is exactly what the criterion asks.

**A defect sitting beside its own correct implementation is far more likely an oversight than a
design decision**, and it shows whoever fixes it what the fix looks like. Always check whether the
sibling branch already does the right thing, and say so — it changes both the severity assessment
and the remedy.

## A RED needs a negative control, or the fix can be over-broad and still pass

#10 (2026-09-18). Its pacing RED was 3 tests / 1 pass / 2 fail, and **the pass was the control**: an
**unconfigured** coordinator must behave exactly as before.

Without that arm, both failing subjects would have been satisfied by an implementation that
**defers unconditionally** — which would have silently changed behaviour for every caller that
opted into nothing. The two reds only mean "the budget was never consulted" *because* a third test
pins what must not change.

**So a RED set has two obligations, not one:** the failing arms show the defect is present, and a
**negative control** shows the space of passing implementations is not wider than intended. The
first stops you shipping nothing; the second stops you shipping too much.

This is the two-failure-directions rule moved from guards to fixes: **before implementing, ask what
over-broad repair would satisfy your failing tests, and add the arm that rejects it.**

## Opt-in options and the seam that already exists

Two design choices worth copying from the same change:

- **Make new pacing opt-in.** Absent the option, the lane behaves exactly as before, so no existing
  caller's timing changes and the blast radius is the callers you deliberately wire.
- **Defer through the seam that already schedules**, and clear the deadline when a frame is
  **deferred** rather than when it **resumes** — so a throwing scheduler cannot leave a spent
  deadline behind. A yield that schedules nothing is a hang; route it through the existing timer
  seam so the liveness guard can see it.
- **Pass the constant the other module already exports** rather than writing a second literal. Two
  literals for one budget is a divergence waiting to happen, and it is invisible until they differ.

## An instrument that perturbs the system it measures

The seventh variant, and the worst one for this epic, because the ordinary remedy makes it worse.

- **A measurement is only as good as your account of what the act of measuring changed.** An e2e
  spec opened a terminal tab per run and did not close it. Tabs accumulated toward the terminal
  residency cap; past a point newly created terminals stopped going live and the new-tab control
  went `disabled`. Every later reading drifted, and each failure read as a fresh product signal
  because each run had altered the conditions of the next — including the one run that passed,
  which happened to execute while the count was still low.
- **Repetition is the normal remedy for a doubtful measurement, and here it advanced the cause.**
  If repeating a measurement changes the result monotonically, suspect the measurement before the
  product.
- **Prefer a probe that needs no state.** Reading which renderer is attached needs neither a new
  session nor terminal input, so the spec that does neither has nothing to leak and nothing to
  perturb. Reach for the reading that requires the least setup, not the most realistic one.

## Operational: leaked terminal sessions on the shared 2222 instance

Found by exhausting the tab cap on the shared verification instance. Costly to rediscover.

- **Deleting a session through `DELETE /api/sessions/:id` alone leaves an orphan tab in the UI.**
  The tab cannot be closed from the UI — its close handler targets a session that no longer
  exists — and it keeps consuming the terminal residency cap. Once the cap is reached the `+`
  control goes `disabled` and no new terminal can be created by anyone using that instance.
- **Delete the tab record too:** `DELETE /api/workspaces/:workspaceId/tabs/:tabId`. Both, in that
  order, or you trade a leaked session for a leaked tab.
- **An e2e spec that creates sessions must close them,** and best-effort cleanup in the spec is not
  enough — a spec that fails before its cleanup still leaks. Prefer a spec that creates nothing.
- **Ownership still applies.** Identify what you created by creation timestamp and name before
  deleting anything, and never delete by name prefix. The pre-existing user session on this
  instance predates the lane by days and is trivially distinguishable from a lane's own debris.

## A control that shares state with the arm it validates is not a control

A paste probe ran two arms in one shell session: the measurement (paste text ending in a newline,
press nothing) and the positive control (paste, then press Enter, to prove pastes arrive at all).
Both files appeared, which read as "the trailing newline auto-executed". It had not. Bracketed
paste had held the first paste on the line un-submitted; the control's Enter then submitted a line
buffer holding **both** pasted commands. Reporting that reading would have reversed a security
default on the strength of an artefact.

- **Sequential arms in one session are not independent when state accumulates between them.** A
  shell line buffer, a scrollback, a clipboard and a residency counter are all such state.
- **Run the negative arm alone, and read the intermediate state rather than the end state.** The
  question was answered by dropping to the DOM renderer and reading the command sitting unexecuted
  on the prompt — not by looking for a side effect afterwards.
- **A positive control proves your mechanism works; it does not license running it in the same
  session as the thing it validates.** Give it its own session, or run it after the measurement is
  already recorded.
- **The control worked correctly, and that is what produced the false positive.** Bracketed paste
  held the first paste unsubmitted exactly as designed; the control's Enter then submitted a buffer
  holding both commands. So a reader checking "did the control pass?" would have been reassured by
  the very thing that corrupted the result. A passing control is not evidence that the measurement
  around it is sound.
- **Name the state, because nobody thinks of a prompt line as state.** The general form of this
  rule sounds like ordinary hygiene and gets skipped. The specific form — a shell line buffer
  accumulates across arms — is what makes it actionable.
- **Prefer the probe that observes over the probe that concludes.** A side-effect probe can only
  see the union of everything that has happened; an intermediate-state probe sees the moment. When
  arms can contaminate each other, read the intermediate state.
- **When the obvious in-system probe is unavailable, reach outside the system rather than for a
  weaker one inside it.** With WebGL attached the terminal text is not in the DOM at all, so the
  natural in-browser check did not exist; making the pasted payload a `touch` moved the question to
  the filesystem, where it was decidable without reading the terminal.

## The shared verification host is a variable, and whoever changes it owns saying so

The multi-lane form of the perturbing-instrument defect above.

- A long-lived server on 2222 held that port, so `SEC-MCP-001 AC-6` could not bind it and the
  monolithic runner read 540/541 rather than 541. The count was an artefact of another agent's
  change to the shared host, not a regression, and the lane measuring it had no way to know.
- **Announce before starting, stopping or restarting anything on 2222**, and say so to whoever is
  measuring against it. A count that moves because the host changed is indistinguishable, from
  inside the measurement, from a count that moved because the code did.
- **Report such a count with its cause attached** — "540/541, the one being another agent's
  listener on 2222" — rather than rounding it to 541 or filing it as a regression.

## Two places the fair-scheduler digest lives

Costly on the next re-seal, and the failure text names neither.

- After re-sealing `docs/analysis/terminal-fairness-authority/`, `FairSchedulerSourceProvenanceRuntime`
  and `FairSchedulerEvidenceBundleRuntime` **stay red until `npm run build` runs**, because they
  read `dist/benchmarks/fair-scheduler-source-provenance.json` rather than the source tree. The
  reported reason is the same `decision-artifact-source-digest-mismatch`, so it looks like the
  re-seal failed when it merely has not reached `dist` yet.
- Same species as the traps CLAUDE.md already records — `dist/` lacking `.ts` sources for
  source-reading tests, and `NODE_TEST_CONTEXT` leaking into child runners.
- **Re-seal, then build, then re-run** — in that order.

## Prefer a probe whose wrong answer is impossible, not merely implausible

- A synchronous busy-loop used to measure main-thread cost reported **idle as slower than loaded**.
  What exposed it was the **ordering**, not the magnitude. Had it returned a merely surprising
  number, the temptation is to explain it away — "the machine was busy", "the first run is cold".
  An impossible ordering admits no such story.
- **Design probes so that a broken probe produces a structurally impossible reading rather than an
  unflattering one.** Ordering, monotonicity and conservation are the cheap invariants: loaded
  cannot be faster than idle, a counter cannot go backwards, bytes out cannot exceed bytes in.
- The replacement measured main-thread occupancy directly — long tasks plus animation-frame
  intervals — rather than inferring it from how long unrelated work took.

## Deleting a session does not mean the server let go of it

One level below the orphan-tab finding, same shape, and it invalidated a measurement.

- `cleanup.mode` is `observe`, with `completed: 0` and `unverifiedSkipped: 35`. `DELETE
  /api/sessions/:id` removes the **record**; it does not reap the PTY. The session disappears from
  the workspace API while its `bash` child keeps running and keeps costing.
- **`/health` returning 200 does not mean the host recovered.** After one overload a health probe
  answered 200 while seven diagnostic PTYs were still live, RSS was climbing past 2 GB and
  `TerminalObs` still reported `totalSessions: 8`. **Verify recovery by the thing that broke** —
  session count, RSS trend, CPU — not by the absence of a new symptom.
- Any measurement taken after someone else intervened in the host is not a clean observation of
  the host. Say who touched it and when, or do not report the number.


## Choose assertions that cannot be true of both the fixed and the defective behaviour

The positive form of nearly every instrument defect in this epic. Each of them was a test that
passed under the defect and under the fix alike.

- Fixing a swallowed clipboard failure, the obvious assertion is **"the handler did not throw"** —
  which is true of the swallow too, and would have shipped green over the unfixed code. The
  assertions used instead were that the fallback does **not** run when the primary succeeds, that a
  double failure **throws**, and that idle, copied and failed are three **mutually distinct**
  states. None of those can hold for the defective version.
- Ask of every new assertion: *what does this print if I revert the fix?* If the answer is "the
  same thing", the assertion is decoration.
- This is the same test as the fail-closed proof fixtures and the mutation checks elsewhere in the
  brief, stated so it can be applied before writing the test rather than audited afterwards.

## A product can be unable to show a defect, exactly as an instrument can be unable to see one

- `MetadataRow` implemented copy failure as **staying idle**, so the failure screen and the
  never-clicked screen were the same screen. The user pressed copy, saw what they always see, and
  pasted the clipboard's previous contents.
- Every other variant in this brief is an instrument that could not see a defect. This is the same
  shape on the other side of the glass: a product that could not show one. When auditing a failure
  path, ask what the user sees on failure and whether it differs from what they see when nothing
  happened at all.
- **Absence of a success indicator is not a failure indicator.** Three states — idle, success,
  failure — need three renderings, and the type should make collapsing them impossible.
